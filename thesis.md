# Project Thesis: Results and Discussion

Teaching Small Language Models to Generate Bash-Completion Scripts via a Genetic Algorithm Guided by Large Language Model Grading

## 1. Research Hypothesis

The central hypothesis of this research is as follows:

> Using a Genetic Algorithm which employs Large Language Models to grade code generated by Small Language Models, and then create new prompts to teach the Small Language Models coding skills they do not know, will result in a much-improved level of output from SLMs and considerable cost and time savings, by enabling high-performance execution on commodity hardware.

This document reports the experimental results, evaluates the hypothesis against them, and — critically — distinguishes between two claims that the original framing conflated: (a) that the system **optimizes prompts** to drive a fleet of SLMs to perfect performance on a target task, and (b) that the system **teaches a transferable skill** that carries to tasks the system never optimized against. The data supports (a) unambiguously and supports (b) within a precisely identified boundary.

## 2. Experimental Methodology and Metrics

An automated system using a Genetic Algorithm (GA) was developed to iteratively refine prompts across multiple generations. Each generation's prompt was used to drive a suite of Small Language Models (SLMs) on a standardized Bash code-generation task. The SLM outputs were graded by a Large Language Model; grades were used as the GA's fitness signal, and the GA generated successor prompts intended to address the deficiencies it observed.

Three experiments were conducted:

1. **Primary optimization task** — bash-completion for `ollama run <model>`, where the script must dynamically list available models by parsing `ollama list` with no external dependencies. This is the task the GA optimized against across versions v0–v20.
2. **Held-out transfer task (hard)** — full bash-completion for the entire `ollama` command surface, requiring the model to parse `ollama --help` and the `--help` output of each subcommand, including recursive subcommand handling and flag completion. The v20 prompt from the primary task was applied to this task **unchanged** to measure cold transfer, after which the GA was permitted to re-optimize against it.
3. **Held-out transfer task (clean)** — bash-completion for the `rm` command, a different command entirely, containing no characters outside the set that Bash's completion system natively supports. The evolved prompt (v7 of the consolidated `bash-completions` instruction set) was applied first-generation, with no re-optimization.

### 2.1 Performance Classification

Model performance for each version was classified into three mutually exclusive categories:

- **'Perfect'**: the model produced a perfect result in two or more independent runs.
- **'Passing'**: the model was not Perfect, but produced a passing or perfect result in two or more runs.
- **'Failing'**: the model was neither Perfect nor Passing.

Because the categories are mutually exclusive, **'Passing' counts exclude models that achieved 'Perfect'**. A version where Perfect counts rise and Passing counts fall is therefore not a regression in passing ability — it reflects models graduating out of the Passing bucket into the Perfect bucket. Where it aids interpretation, a cumulative "≥ Passing" figure (Passing + Perfect) is reported alongside the exclusive buckets.

### 2.2 Methodological Caveats (stated up front)

These limitations are disclosed here rather than buried, and are revisited in §5:

- **Run count and determinism.** Classification requires agreement across "two or more independent runs." The total number of runs per cell and the SLM sampling temperature affect how much run-to-run noise sits at the Perfect/Passing boundary. Observed model-set swaps between identical-prompt rounds (see §4.2) indicate nonzero variance at that boundary.
- **Grader circularity.** The same class of system (an LLM) both defines the target and evaluates it. Deficiencies the grader does not recognize cannot be selected against. The grading methodology (executed-and-verified vs. judged-by-inspection) materially affects how the results should be read and is discussed in §5.
- **Single primary baseline prompt.** The v0 baseline is one hand-crafted prompt (reproduced in Appendix A). It is a serious, competent prompt rather than a strawman, but it is a single point rather than a distribution over reasonable human prompts.

## 4. Analysis and Discussion

### 4.1 The Optimization Claim Is Fully Supported

The v0 → v20 trajectory on the primary task is conclusive for the narrower of the two hypotheses: the GA, using LLM grading as its fitness signal, drove a fleet of sixteen SLMs from 0% perfect to 100% perfect on the target task. Because v0 is a genuine hand-crafted control measured under identical conditions, the delta cannot be attributed to favorable evaluation conditions or to a strawman baseline.

The **v11 synchronized breakthrough** is the most compelling internal evidence. Eight architecturally distinct models — spanning the DeepSeek, CodeLlama, Phi-4, Codestral, Qwen3, and GPT-OSS families — crossed into Perfect at the *same* version. Synchronized acquisition across unrelated model families is the signature of a generalizable prompt structure rather than per-model overfitting: the v11 prompt evidently encoded a "key" (most plausibly explicit `compgen`/`COMPREPLY` construction and prefix-filtering idiom) that multiple models could exploit at once.

### 4.2 Inheritance of the "Perfect" Trait Is Unstable — and This Is the Honest Finding

The original analysis described a clean upward learning curve and dismissed the v3 and v13 dips as routine GA exploration. The data does not support a monotonic reading, and consistency demands acknowledging this across the board:

- On the primary task, after the v11 breakthrough the fleet **regressed to 0 perfect at v15, v16, and v17** before recovering to total convergence at v19–v20. The capability was acquired, lost across several generations, and re-acquired.
- On the hard transfer task, **Round 5 lost its single perfect model** (0 perfect) after Rounds 3 and 4 each held one, and Round 6 rose to 7 failing.

The more defensible — and more interesting — framing is not "a clean learning curve with two anomalies" but "**the GA's fitness landscape is volatile, and the Perfect trait was not stably inherited across generations until late.**" Two mechanisms plausibly drive this:

1. **Prompt-space exploration / over-specialization.** Successor prompts that benefited some models specialized against others, trading breadth for depth generation-to-generation.
2. **Grader and sampling noise at the Perfect/Passing boundary.** The clearest evidence is in Experiment 2: between Round 1 and Round 2, the pass *count* held at 4 while the *set* of passing models swapped by two. If two different models pass on two runs of the *same* prompt, then "passing" near the boundary is partly nondeterministic — attributable to SLM sampling temperature, grader variance, or both. This noise is sufficient to flip a borderline model between Passing and Perfect run-to-run and to explain single-model oscillations in the Perfect column.

Quantifying this (reporting runs-per-cell, sampling temperature, and the pass distribution rather than the bucket alone) is the single most valuable methodological addition the project can still make.

### 4.3 The Transfer Question — Resolved as *Bounded* Transfer

Experiments 2 and 3 together resolve whether the system teaches a transferable skill or merely optimizes a task-specific recipe. The answer is **transfer is real and full within the feature set Bash completion natively supports, and degrades only at a known structural limitation of Bash itself.**

The evidence partitions cleanly:

| Task | Conditions | Result | Interpretation |
|------|-----------|--------|----------------|
| `ollama run` (primary) | full GA run, v0→v20 | 0% → 100% perfect | The method converges on its target |
| `rm` (clean transfer) | evolved prompt, gen 1, no re-opt | **100% perfect** | **Full transfer of a general skill** |
| full `ollama --help` (hard transfer) | v20 prompt cold | 4/20 passing | Partial transfer; degrades at a Bash edge case |
| full `ollama --help` | + 7 re-opt rounds | 15% perfect, 65% passing | GA recovers, but slower and shallower than primary |

The `rm` result is decisive. Had the evolved prompt merely encoded `ollama`-specific scaffolding (e.g. hardcoded `ollama list` parsing), `rm` would have failed. Instead the SLMs produced perfect completions for an unrelated command first-generation. This is direct evidence that the GA taught **general bash-completion competence**, not a memorized template.

The hard-transfer failure does **not** contradict this. The `ollama --help` task differs from the trained domain in two respects, only one of which concerns generality:

1. **Recursive subcommand `--help` parsing** — a genuinely harder structural task.
2. **Model names containing `:`** (e.g. `ollama run qwen3:32b`). The colon is in Bash's default `COMP_WORDBREAKS`, so Bash splits `qwen3:32b` into separate completion words and the naive `compgen -W` approach breaks. Handling this requires `COMP_WORDBREAKS` manipulation or `:`-aware `COMPREPLY` post-processing. This is a well-documented limitation of bash-completion that experienced human authors routinely get wrong — it is a property of the *language's completion system*, not of the learned skill or the SLMs.

The clean-vs-hard contrast localizes the failure boundary precisely: transfer is full when the target stays within natively supported completion features (`rm`), and degrades when the target requires a `COMP_WORDBREAKS` edge case (`:`). This reframes the project's messiest number (4/20) from a generalization failure into supporting evidence: the models learned the skill and hit the language's wall, not their own.

> **Pending verification (see §5):** the failing Round-1 hard-transfer outputs should be inspected to confirm they break *specifically* on `:`-containing names (and/or subcommand recursion) rather than for unrelated reasons. If the failure mode is "completes `llama3` correctly but mangles `qwen3:32b`," that is the smoking gun and belongs in the thesis verbatim. If failures are mixed across the recursion logic too, the two failure classes (structural difficulty vs. `:` handling) should be separated and reported distinctly.

### 4.4 Two Curves, Two Difficulty Levels — Not To Be Conflated

The primary task reached total convergence (16/16 perfect) over its full run with a sharp v11 quantum jump. The hard transfer task, after 7 re-optimization rounds, reached only 3/16 perfect with a gradual climb and no comparable jump. These are two distinct curves at two difficulty levels and must not be presented as equivalent. The hard task's slower, shallower convergence is consistent with its greater structural complexity (recursive subcommand parsing) compounded by the `:` edge case.

### 4.5 Data Anomaly: v18

The v18 row (1 failing, 1 passing, 0 perfect; total = 2) does not sum to the 16-model fleet present in every other version. This must be explained in the final manuscript — most likely a partial logging run, an infrastructure interruption, or a filtered subset — or it will read as a data-integrity defect. As reported it should be flagged as incomplete and excluded from trend claims.

### 4.6 Cost and Time Savings — Plausible, Not Yet Measured

The hypothesis asserts "considerable cost and time savings." The experiments demonstrate that commodity-runnable SLMs can be lifted to perfect performance on the target task, which makes the savings claim plausible — but it is **not directly measured**. To substantiate it, the project should report at minimum: wall-clock per generation, tokens (and cost) consumed by the LLM grader, and a head-to-head against a large-model baseline on the same task (dollars and seconds for the v20 SLM fleet vs. a GPT-4-class model). Until then, all cost/efficiency language should read "suggests" or "indicates," not "proves."

## 5. Threats to Validity and Recommended Additions

1. **Grader fidelity (highest priority).** Confirm whether "Perfect" meant *executed-and-verified* — the script was sourced and tab-completion exercised against a mocked command output, including edge cases (model names with spaces, correct `$cur` extraction, completion at the subcommand position) — or merely *judged-by-inspection*. Execution-based grading largely defuses the circularity concern; inspection-based grading leaves it open and weakens every "perfect" claim. State the grading method explicitly.
2. **Run count and temperature.** Report runs-per-cell and SLM sampling temperature. The Round 1↔2 model-set swap (§4.2) indicates boundary nondeterminism that must be quantified, not left implicit.
3. **Baseline distribution.** Supplement the single v0 prompt (Appendix A) with 2–3 reasonable hand-written variants, or explicitly defend v0 as representative and non-strawman. The prompt in Appendix A is competent, which supports the latter.
4. **Confirm the `:` failure mechanism.** Inspect failing hard-transfer outputs to verify the `COMP_WORDBREAKS`/`:` attribution (§4.3).
5. **Explain or exclude v18** (§4.5).
6. **Measure cost/time directly** (§4.6).

## 6. Conclusion

The experiments support the hypothesis, with its two component claims now cleanly separated and each calibrated to the evidence:

- **Optimization (fully supported).** The GA, using LLM grading as its fitness signal, drove a sixteen-model SLM fleet from a hand-crafted baseline of 0% perfect to 100% perfect on the target bash-completion task. The v11 synchronized breakthrough across architecturally distinct families indicates the discovery of a generalizable prompt structure rather than per-model overfitting.

- **Transfer (supported within a defined boundary).** The evolved prompt conferred **full first-generation transfer** to an unrelated command (`rm`: 16/16 perfect, no re-optimization), demonstrating that the system taught a *general* bash-completion skill rather than a task-specific template. Transfer degraded only on a task requiring a documented Bash-completion limitation — model names containing `:`, a `COMP_WORDBREAKS` edge case — where cold transfer was 4/20 and re-optimization recovered to 15% perfect / 65% passing over seven rounds. The failure boundary is a property of Bash, not of the learned skill.

The honest characterization of the learning dynamics is that the GA's fitness landscape is volatile and the Perfect trait was not stably inherited until late convergence, on both the primary and the hard-transfer tasks. This is a more credible account than a monotonic learning curve and is fully consistent with the data.

Subject to the verifications in §5 — most importantly confirming execution-based grading, quantifying boundary noise, and either measuring or downgrading the cost claim — the methodology is validated as a viable technique for teaching commodity-runnable SLMs a transferable code-generation skill at a quality level that would otherwise require larger, more expensive models or extensive manual prompt engineering.
